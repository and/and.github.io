A Timer You Don't Have to Read

A digital timer makes you stop, look and do arithmetic. An hourglass tells you the same thing at a glance — and this one sits on your desktop, free, above whatever you are working in.

Reading as

Look at 07:41 on a countdown and you have to read it, parse it, and work out what it means. Look at an hourglass and you already know: about a third gone, plenty left. Your eye does it without interrupting whatever you were thinking about.

That is the whole idea. Here is one — flip it, drop it, shake it, drag it around. It behaves the way a real one does, which is what makes it readable at a glance.

Using it

Click it to start. Click again to pause — it tips onto its side, the way you'd lay a real one down — and once more to carry on. Drag it anywhere on screen; let go and it drops to the bottom and settles. Right-click for how long it should run: anything from 1 to 60 minutes, with 25 for a Pomodoro and 6- and 12-minute blocks if you bill in tenths of an hour.

It sits above your other windows without ever taking focus, on whichever desktop you're on, and it has no Dock icon to clutter anything. If you'd rather it were out of sight, hide it in the menu bar and the time left shows up there instead, where you can pause, resume and restart it.

It stays quiet unless you ask it not to: the falling sand makes no sound until you turn it up, and the only thing you'll hear by default is a chime when your time is up. There's an optional soft chime each minute if you like knowing time is moving without looking.

It remembers what you actually did

Not what you planned, and not what you started — only the time the sand was genuinely running. Open Statistics from the menu and you get your day, your week, your month and your year, with a bar for each and the weekday under it, so a fortnight of work shows you its own shape: which days you got going, which ones you didn't, whether your weekends are really off.

All of it stays on your Mac. Nothing is uploaded, there's no account, and the export is there for when you want the numbers somewhere else — an invoice, a timesheet, or a spreadsheet of your own.

If you use Claude, the app ships a small server it can read that record through, so you can ask "how much did I focus this week?" without opening anything. It can start and pause the timer for you too, once you turn that on — it's off until you do.

Why it behaves like sand

A timer you read at a glance only works if the glass is honest: half the sand gone should look like half the sand gone. That's why the sand isn't an animation. It's computed, and it's worth a paragraph on what that means — at whatever depth you feel like.

Sand keeps steadier time than water. Water empties fast when the bottle is full and slowly at the end, because the water above presses down. Sand doesn't: grains lean on each other and on the glass, so the sand at the neck barely feels the weight above it and trickles out at the same rate throughout. That steadiness is the only reason an hourglass can tell you anything at all.

Two more things you can watch for above. A heap of sand always slumps to the same slope, so the dip in the top and the cone in the bottom keep matching angles. And if you drop the glass, the stream stops in mid-air — the sand is falling as fast as the glass is, so relative to the glass it goes nowhere.

Water drains at a speed set by the depth above the hole (Torricelli's law), which is useless for a clock. Grains form force chains that carry weight into the walls instead, so the pressure at the outlet stops growing once the column is a couple of widths deep. The rate becomes a property of the opening, not of the amount above it.

That leads to the one rule with a visible consequence: flow scales with roughly the 2.5 power of the opening's width — an area (∝ D²) moving at a speed set by grains falling about a hole-width (∝ √D). Hold the sand fixed and invert it, and the neck has to follow the duration:

rate ∝ width2.5 flow through the opening
rate ∝ 1 / minutes same sand, longer run
width ∝ minutes−0.4 so a short timer gets a fatter neck

Sixty times slower is only about five times narrower, which is why the 1-minute and the 60-minute glass above look more alike than you'd expect. The heaps are solved from volume rather than drawn: given the sand left, find the cone that displaces exactly that much, at the angle a real heap holds. Shake it and they flatten, and new sand builds on the flattened layer instead of resuming the old shape.

The empirical form is Beverloo's law, W = C·ρb·√g·(D − k·d)2.5, with C ≈ 0.58 and k ≈ 1.4; the (D − k·d) correction — a grain's centre can't sit closer than its radius to the edge — matters only when the opening is a few grains across. The head-independence is Janssen's 1895 result: wall friction takes up the load and pressure saturates over a length of order D. The √g is why a dropped glass doesn't merely slow down: at free fall the force chains go slack and the flow stops outright.

There is no contact model here — it's the continuum story, enough for an honest-looking glass and nowhere near enough to predict a jam, which is the interesting failure mode: an opening a few grains wide arches over and stops entirely.

Two deliberate departures, both for looks: the neck is clamped at the extremes, because a true 1-minute throat stops reading as an hourglass (real short timers use coarser sand instead), and long timers narrow only half as far, or the stream would be too fine to see. And the countdown itself comes from the clock, not the simulation — the sand decides how it looks, a timestamp decides when it finishes, so it is exactly as accurate as your Mac's clock.
